Read an 835 without double-counting: a two-claim walkthrough

By Visera / Jovanie Rosario, Focus Care LLC. I am affiliated with the viewer mentioned below. This article was drafted with AI assistance and checked against the linked references; its numbers and claim identifiers are invented.

An 835 reader can produce a neat table and still leave you with the wrong total. A common trap is to combine claim totals, service amounts and adjustment detail as though every number were an additional transaction.

A useful first exercise is to define what a small remittance excerpt means before trying to convert it into rows. Here is a two-claim example you can inspect in a text editor, followed by three checks that keep its meaning intact.

This is a teaching fragment, not a complete X12 transaction or a payer submission. It omits envelopes, payment information and other required context. No patient, payer or provider information comes from a real transaction. Do not upload this fragment expecting a complete-file parser to accept it.

Build a small example with an answer you can check

In this fragment, * separates elements and ~ ends each segment. These are the delimiters chosen for this example, not a rule to hard-code for every X12 file.

CLP*VISERA-EXAMPLE-A*1*125*90*10*12*SYNTHETIC-A~
CAS*CO*45*25~
CAS*PR*1*10~
CLP*VISERA-EXAMPLE-B*1*80*0*0*12*SYNTHETIC-B~
CAS*CO*29*80~

Read the two CLP segments as the starts of two claim records. In each one, the claim reference follows the segment identifier, then the status, charge, payment and patient-responsibility amounts. SYNTHETIC-A and SYNTHETIC-B are invented payer claim control references.

The first claim reports a $125 charge and $90 paid. Its adjustment detail has $25 under CO/45 and $10 under PR/1. The second reports an $80 charge and no payment, with an $80 CO/29 adjustment.

Both use claim status1, processed as primary. “Processed” does not mean “paid”: a processed claim can have zero payment. X12 limits status4 to an unrecognized patient/subscriber when the claim was not forwarded, so status4 is not a generic marker for a filing-limit denial. X12's official clarification.

The field roles are documented in CMS's 835 field reference. The fragment is deliberately narrow: it illustrates claim-level adjustments, not every possible claim status, benefit arrangement or service-line layout.

Before building a report, write the expected claim table:

Claim Charge Payment Reported patient responsibility
VISERA-EXAMPLE-A $125 $90 $10
VISERA-EXAMPLE-B $80 $0 $0
Total $205 $90 $10

Keep the adjustment detail as a related table:

Claim Level in this fragment Group Reason Amount
VISERA-EXAMPLE-A Claim CO 45 $25
VISERA-EXAMPLE-A Claim PR 1 $10
VISERA-EXAMPLE-B Claim CO 29 $80

That gives a concrete expected result: two claims, three adjustment entries and $90 in claim payments.

Check 1: preserve the relationship between a claim and its adjustments

The two CAS segments after claim A belong to that claim in this excerpt. The CAS segment after claim B belongs to B.

A flattened export can accidentally repeat a claim's payment once per adjustment:

claim,paid,group,reason,adjustment
VISERA-EXAMPLE-A,90,CO,45,25
VISERA-EXAMPLE-A,90,PR,1,10
VISERA-EXAMPLE-B,0,CO,29,80

Summing the paid column now produces $180. Nothing in the remittance paid claim A twice. The export repeated the claim-level value to describe two related adjustments.

Two related tables avoid that ambiguity. If you need one flat table for review, label repeated claim totals clearly and aggregate payments from one record per claim. Preserve the source transaction and claim references so another reviewer can trace a row back to the remittance.

The same design question appears with service lines. Adding a service table is not permission to add its payments again to the claim payment total. Decide which level your report is measuring.

Check 2: do not add patient responsibility twice

For claim A, this example's arithmetic is:

$125 charge = $90 payment + $25 CO adjustment + $10 PR adjustment

The $10 in the CLP patient-responsibility field and the $10 PR adjustment describe the same responsibility amount in this simplified example. Adding both as separate reductions would produce a false $135 explanation of a $125 charge.

Read group and reason together. X12 identifies CO as contractual obligation and PR as patient responsibility. Its current reason list describes reason 1 as a deductible; reason 45 concerns a charge exceeding an applicable allowance or fee arrangement; reason 29 concerns an expired filing limit. Reason 45 also has usage restrictions, including that its amount must not equal the entire claim or service charge. Our $25 example is smaller than the $125 charge. X12 group codes, X12 reason codes and usage notes.

These labels describe the payer's reported adjustment. They are not an instruction to bill a patient, write off a balance or file an appeal. For a real follow-up, read the full remittance, applicable remarks and payer instructions, and check the existing account activity.

A useful work item would name the claim, the CO/29 combination and the $80 amount, then state what needs investigation. “Please review the denial” leaves the next person guessing.

Check 3: keep the claim sum separate from the actual payment

Our two claim payments add to $90. That is all this fragment establishes about payment totals.

It contains no BPR, TRN or PLB segment. We therefore cannot use it to prove a $90 bank deposit or reconcile a complete transaction.

In a full file, BPR describes payment information and TRN supplies reassociation trace information. Provider-level adjustments may explain differences that do not belong to one current claim. CMS distinguishes service, claim and provider-level adjustments, and separately explains how ERA trace information is used to match the associated payment. CMS remittance overview, CMS EFT/ERA basics.

If your result disagrees with a payment record, first confirm the transaction and reference you are comparing. Look for provider-level adjustments, reversals and additional transactions before forcing a total to match.

Practice with a complete synthetic file

A small excerpt is useful for checking an interpretation. A complete synthetic file is better for checking an importer, because it can include envelopes, control numbers, payment information and repeatable expected results.

For each fixture, record the expected claim count, claim payments, adjustment level and any provider-level differences. Then check both the summary and an individual claim. A successful parse does not prove that every identifier, date, code or reconciliation case is handled correctly.
